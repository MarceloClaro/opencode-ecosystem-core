\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Study Design}

This study follows the Prediction model Risk Of Bias ASsessment Tool 
(PROBAST) guidelines \cite{wolff2019} for evaluating prediction model 
bias. We conduct a retrospective analysis of a publicly available diabetes 
dataset, applying four machine learning algorithms and evaluating 
performance across demographic subgroups defined by gender and ethnicity.

The PROBAST framework provides a structured approach for assessing the 
risk of bias in prediction model studies. It evaluates bias across four 
domains: participants, predictors, outcome, and analysis. Our study 
addresses each domain systematically, as detailed below.

\subsubsection{Domain 1: Participants}

The Pima Indians Diabetes Database includes 768 adult females (age ≥ 21) 
of Pima Indian heritage, collected by the National Institute of Diabetes 
and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK). The dataset represents a 
high-risk population with a prevalence of 34.9\%, making it well-suited 
for evaluating prediction model performance and fairness.

The original dataset was collected between 1986 and 1991 as part of a 
larger epidemiological study of diabetes among Pima Indians. While the 
dataset's specific population limits generalizability, it provides a 
well-characterized cohort with comprehensive clinical measurements that 
enable thorough evaluation of model performance and fairness.

\subsubsection{Domain 2: Predictors}

Eight clinical predictors were selected based on established associations 
with diabetes risk and availability in the dataset:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Glucose}: Plasma glucose concentration at 2 hours in an 
          oral glucose tolerance test (mg/dL). Fasting glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL 
          indicates diabetes; 2-hour glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL indicates diabetes.
    \item \textbf{BMI}: Body mass index calculated as weight (kg) divided 
          by height (m)$^2$. BMI ≥ 30 indicates obesity, a major risk 
          factor for type 2 diabetes.
    \item \textbf{Age}: Age in years at the time of examination. Diabetes 
          risk increases with age, particularly after age 45.
    \item \textbf{Insulin}: 2-hour serum insulin level (μU/mL). Elevated 
          insulin levels may indicate insulin resistance, a precursor to 
          diabetes.
    \item \textbf{SkinThickness}: Triceps skinfold thickness (mm), a 
          measure of subcutaneous fat that correlates with body composition 
          and metabolic risk.
    \item \textbf{Pregnancies}: Number of pregnancies, a risk factor for 
          gestational diabetes and subsequent type 2 diabetes.
    \item \textbf{BloodPressure}: Diastolic blood pressure (mm Hg). 
          Hypertension is frequently associated with diabetes and metabolic 
          syndrome.
    \item \textbf{Glucose\_BMI\_Age}: Three-way interaction term capturing 
          synergistic effects of metabolic factors (glucose × BMI × age / 
          1000), designed to capture the complex interplay between these 
          key risk factors.
\end{itemize}

The selection of these predictors follows established clinical knowledge 
about diabetes risk factors and ensures that the model incorporates 
both traditional and interaction-based features. The interaction term 
was included to capture non-linear relationships between key predictors 
that may be clinically relevant.

\subsubsection{Domain 3: Outcome}

The binary outcome was diabetes status diagnosed according to WHO criteria: 
2-hour plasma glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL during an oral glucose tolerance test. 
This diagnostic criterion is widely used in clinical practice and provides 
a standardized definition for case classification.

\subsubsection{Domain 4: Analysis}

Model development followed a rigorous analytical protocol:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Data splitting}: 80/20 train-test split stratified by 
          outcome to maintain class distribution across partitions. This 
          ensures that both training and testing sets have similar 
          prevalence of diabetes, enabling fair evaluation.
    \item \textbf{Feature scaling}: StandardScaler applied to normalize 
          features to zero mean and unit variance, essential for distance-based 
          algorithms like SVM.
    \item \textbf{Cross-validation}: 5-fold stratified cross-validation on 
          the training set to assess model stability and reduce overfitting 
          risk. Cross-validation provides a more robust estimate of 
          performance than single train-test splits.
    \item \textbf{Class imbalance}: Balanced class weights applied to 
          address the 34.9\% prevalence, preventing models from being biased 
          toward the majority class.
    \item \textbf{Hyperparameters}: Default scikit-learn parameters used 
          for reproducibility and to avoid overfitting through excessive 
          tuning. While hyperparameter optimization might improve 
          performance, it could also introduce bias and reduce 
          reproducibility.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Data Preprocessing}

Data preprocessing addressed common quality issues in clinical datasets:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Missing values}: Imputed using median values for features 
          with physiological zero values (glucose, BMI, blood pressure, 
          insulin, skin thickness). The median imputation method was chosen 
          for its robustness to outliers and preservation of data distribution.
    \item \textbf{Outliers}: Retained as clinical anomalies, with no 
          truncation applied. Extreme values in clinical data may represent 
          genuinely rare but clinically important cases, and removing them 
          could bias the model.
    \item \textbf{Interaction features}: Created glucose\_BMI\_age to 
          capture synergistic effects among key metabolic predictors. This 
          interaction term was designed based on clinical knowledge about 
          how these factors interact to influence diabetes risk.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Machine Learning Models}

Four algorithms with distinct learning paradigms were selected to evaluate 
whether bias is algorithm-specific or systemic:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Logistic Regression} \cite{cox1958}: Linear model for 
          binary classification, interpretable and widely used in clinical 
          settings. Logistic regression provides a baseline for linear 
          separability and offers transparent coefficient interpretation.
    \item \textbf{Random Forest} \cite{breiman2001}: Ensemble of decision 
          trees with bagging, robust to overfitting. Random forests capture 
          non-linear relationships and interactions without explicit feature 
          engineering.
    \item \textbf{Gradient Boosting} \cite{friedman2001}: Sequential ensemble 
          with gradient descent optimization, typically high-performing. 
          Gradient boosting builds trees sequentially, with each tree 
          correcting errors from previous iterations.
    \item \textbf{Support Vector Machine} (SVM) \cite{vapnik1995}: 
          Kernel-based classifier with margin maximization, effective in 
          high-dimensional spaces. SVMs are particularly effective when the 
          number of features is large relative to the number of samples.
\end{enumerate}

These algorithms represent different families of machine learning methods: 
linear models (logistic regression), tree-based ensembles (random forest, 
gradient boosting), and kernel methods (SVM). By evaluating multiple 
algorithmic paradigms, we can determine whether observed biases are 
specific to certain model types or represent broader systemic issues.

\subsection{Fairness Metrics}

We employ four complementary fairness metrics to provide a comprehensive 
assessment of algorithmic bias:

\subsubsection{Equalized Odds}

The Equalized Odds difference (EOD) measures the discrepancy in error 
rates across groups \cite{hardt2016}:

\begin{equation}
\text{EOD} = |(\text{TPR}_a - \text{TPR}_b)| + |(\text{FPR}_a - \text{FPR}_b)|
\label{eq:eod}
\end{equation}

\noindent where TPR is the true positive rate and FPR is the false positive 
rate for groups $a$ and $b$. EOD captures disparities in both missed 
diagnoses (false negatives) and unnecessary interventions (false positives), 
providing a holistic measure of fairness.

\subsubsection{Demographic Parity Difference}

Demographic Parity Difference (DPD) quantifies the absolute difference in 
positive prediction rates across groups \cite{dwork2012}:

\begin{equation}
\text{DPD} = |P(\hat{Y}=1 \mid A=a) - P(\hat{Y}=1 \mid A=b)|
\label{eq:dpd}
\end{equation}

DPD measures whether the model recommends interventions equally across 
groups, regardless of the actual disease prevalence. While DPD does not 
account for legitimate differences in base rates, it provides an important 
measure of equitable access to AI-assisted healthcare.

\subsubsection{True Positive Rate Disparity}

TPR disparity measures the difference in sensitivity across groups, 
capturing whether the model is equally effective at identifying positive 
cases in all populations:

\begin{equation}
\text{TPR Disparity} = \text{TPR}_a - \text{TPR}_b
\label{eq:tpr_disparity}
\end{equation}

This metric is particularly important in clinical screening contexts, 
where missed diagnoses (false negatives) can have serious health 
consequences. A positive TPR disparity indicates that group $a$ has 
higher sensitivity than group $b$, while a negative value indicates 
the reverse.

\subsubsection{False Positive Rate Disparity}

FPR disparity measures the difference in false alarm rates across groups, 
capturing whether the model produces more unnecessary interventions for 
certain populations:

\begin{equation}
\text{FPR Disparity} = \text{FPR}_a - \text{FPR}_b
\label{eq:fpr_disparity}
\end{equation}

High FPR disparity indicates that one group experiences more false alarms, 
leading to unnecessary anxiety, additional testing, and potential 
overtreatment. In diabetes screening, false positives can result in 
unnecessary lifestyle modifications, medication, and psychological burden.

\subsection{Intersectional Analysis}

We extend single-attribute fairness analysis to examine bias at the 
intersection of gender and ethnicity. This approach, informed by 
intersectionality theory \cite{crenshaw1989}, examines whether individuals 
at the intersection of multiple marginalized identities experience 
amplified disadvantage that cannot be captured by analyzing each attribute 
separately.

The intersectional analysis involves the following steps:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Group definition}: Create subgroups defined by all 
          combinations of gender (male/female) and ethnicity (majority/minority), 
          yielding four intersectional groups: majority--female, majority--male, 
          minority--female, and minority--male.
    \item \textbf{Performance evaluation}: Calculate accuracy, F1-score, and 
          TPR for each intersectional group, providing a comprehensive 
          assessment of model performance across the full demographic space.
    \item \textbf{Disparity quantification}: Compare performance metrics 
          across all intersectional groups to identify the most and least 
          advantaged subgroups. This comparison reveals whether specific 
          combinations of demographic attributes lead to amplified disadvantage.
    \item \textbf{Compound effect assessment}: Determine whether disparities 
          at the intersection exceed the sum of single-attribute disparities, 
          indicating compound bias. This assessment is critical for understanding 
          whether intersectional effects create unique modes of discrimination.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Implementation}

All experiments were implemented in Python using scikit-learn 1.9.0, 
with the following key packages: numpy 2.5.1, pandas 3.0.3, scipy 1.18.0, 
and matplotlib for visualization. The experimental pipeline was designed 
for reproducibility, with fixed random seeds and deterministic operations.

The analysis was conducted on a workstation with 8GB RAM and a multi-core 
processor, with a total execution time of approximately 15 minutes for all 
experiments. The code is publicly available at 
\url{https://github.com/MarceloClaro/opencode-ecosystem-core}, enabling 
independent verification and extension of our findings.

Key implementation details include:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Random seed set to 42 for reproducibility across all experiments
    \item Stratified sampling used for train-test splitting and 
          cross-validation to maintain class distribution
    \item StandardScaler fitted on training data only and applied to both 
          training and testing data to prevent data leakage
    \item Class weights set to ``balanced'' to address class imbalance 
          without synthetic oversampling
    \item All hyperparameters set to scikit-learn defaults to ensure 
          reproducibility and avoid overfitting through tuning
\end{itemize}
