I.  INTRODUCTION 
It’s no secret:  Artificial Intelligence (“AI”), for better or 
worse, is revolutionizing the world.2  The majority of people in 
the United States now interact with AI on a daily basis without 
even realizing it.3  It seems that every company, electronic device, 
app, store, and e-commerce website now offers its own version of 
AI “help” in the form of a chatbot, virtual assistant, product 
recommendation feature, or voice response system (think Siri or 
Alexa) in an attempt to make our lives easier and more efficient.4  
Experts in almost every field are rushing to integrate AI into their 
workstreams and are finding helpful applications for its use in 
fields such as law, education, finance, and healthcare.5   
The legal field and legal education at large are not immune 
to this explosion of possible uses for AI technology.6  A quick 
web search provides results for dozens of new or forthcoming 
applications and programs that promise to help legal professionals 
streamline work that is often tedious and mundane.7  Tired, busy, 
and overburdened lawyers everywhere are likely to salivate at the 
idea of an AI-integrated digital assistant who could sprint through

B. The First ChatGPT Case—Mata v. Avianca 
Among the many recently publicized cases and general 
discussions on AI’s place in the law, the Mata v. Avianca debacle 
stands out as an early watershed moment and helped amplify the 
conversation surrounding the ethical implications of AI use in the 
legal field.48  The case revolved around an attorney’s use of 
ChatGPT, the generative language AI model developed by Open 
AI.49  One of the attorneys representing Mr. Roberto Mata in an 
injury lawsuit against the airline company Avianca, Inc. turned to 
ChatGPT for help in preparing a ten-page brief for the court.50  
Mr. Steven A. Schwartz of Levidow, Levidow & Oberman P.C. 
authored a brief that cited more than half a dozen supporting cases 
containing decisions and outcomes that were (perhaps ironically 
from our vantage point today) almost too good to be true.51  The 
citations included cases such as Martinez v. Delta Air Lines, 
Zicherman v. Korean Air Lines, and Varghese v. China Southern 
Airlines.52  The problem was, however, that each one of these 
perfectly supportive cases was entirely fictitious—100% made 
up.53  ChatGPT churned out party names, fact patterns, relevant 
issues, judicial analyses, and court holdings that were entirely 
hallucinated.54  Counsel for Avianca, Inc. subsequently inquired 
about the cases, as they were unable to locate them in their 
research, and it was ultimately discovered that Mr. Schwartz 
relied entirely upon ChatGPT’s legal knowledge (or lack 
thereof).55  Before submitting his brief to the court, Mr. Schwartz 
never took the time to verify the legitimacy of the supposed case 
law support provided by ChatGPT, much less its existence.56  Mr. 
Schwartz was lambasted in the court of public opinion and 
received fines and sanctions from the presiding judge for his lack

C. Ethical Guidelines and the Future of AI Use in the Law 
The rules of legal ethics and professional responsibility are 
at a fork in the road because of the development of Artificial 
Intelligence and its ongoing merger with the legal community.69  
These rules, deeply rooted in centuries of judicial outcomes, have 
been crafted to uphold the sanctity of the law and a strict code 
emphasizing client welfare, privacy, truthfulness, and honesty.70  
Today, these foundational principles face novel challenges, ones 
that early drafters of the rules could not have envisioned.71 
Consider client confidentiality, a bedrock principle of legal 
practice, essential for upholding clients’ constitutional rights.72  
How do we ensure its inviolability in an age where AI systems 
process sensitive client data, which then may be stored

IV. CONCLUSION 
As explored in this Comment, the integration of Artificial 
Intelligence into the legal domain points to a massive 
transformation in the future practice of law.  However, the ethical 
foundation that has sustained the law through centuries remains 
the same—to respect the legal system and those who serve it, and 
to perform one’s work with competence and integrity.  AI’s 
arrival into a domain traditionally reserved for human intellect 
and discernment necessitates a reevaluation of the fundamental 
tenets of legal ethics, professional responsibility, and legal 
education.   
Mata v. Avianca and cases like it serve as cautionary tales of 
the potential misuses and misapplications of AI technology and 
are equally prescient indicators of the inevitable integration of AI 
in the legal field.233  There is a spotlight on the importance of 
developing broad ethical guidelines and standards that can evolve 
alongside the inevitable advancements of AI technology.234  
These standards cannot be intended as mere speedbumps for 
slowing the rapid changes in legal work and technology but must 
be thoughtfully developed and implemented to ensure that the 
sanctity of the law and its ethical practice is upheld in an 
increasingly automated future.   
Additionally, the imperatives for legal education are clear:  
Law schools must pivot not only to acquaint students with AI but 
also to imbed in them the critical faculties necessary to harness 
AI judiciously.235  This educational revamp is vital to fostering a

Make no mistake—AI is here to stay and will only increase 
in efficiency, accuracy, and competence.  Often, when it comes 
to the topic of integrating AI, the dialogue centers around whether 
we should or should not welcome it or whether it is good or bad—
this analysis is wrong.  The greater integration of Artificial 
Intelligence in the legal domain is inevitable, and the conversation 
must shift to how we embrace it responsibly.
