Generative artificial intelligence vs. law students: an 
empirical study on criminal law exam performance
Armin Alimardani 
School of Law, University of Wollongong, Wollongong, Australia
ABSTRACT
Claims that GPT-4 can outperform more than 90% of human test-takers in the 
US Uniform Bar Examination have sparked heated debates about the impact of 
Generative AI (GenAI) on legal education, academic integrity, and the future of 
legal practice. Yet GenAI’s capabilities in broader legal examination contexts – 
including in jurisdictions outside the US – are unclear. This study addresses this 
gap by evaluating GenAI’s performance against students who took the ‘Criminal 
Law’ final exam at an Australian law school in Spring 2023. Various AI models 
and prompt engineering techniques were used to generate 10 distinct 
answers to the exam question. Five criminal law tutors, unaware of AI 
involvement, graded a mix of AI-generated and student responses. Then, the 
tutors were briefed on the AI-generated papers they marked and engaged in 
reflective semi-structured interviews. The study found that GenAI performed 
below the student average in questions that required detailed legal and 
critical analysis. However, all GenAI papers performed better than students in 
open-ended questions and essay writing tasks. These results provide a 
benchmark for the capabilities and limitations of GenAI in higher education 
and provide insights into the potential implications of its application to legal 
assessments and education, curriculum development, and the future workforce.

2. Methodology
In this study, references to GenAI models may inadvertently suggest anthro­
pomorphism, potentially giving an incorrect impression of the capabilities of 
AI or implying that it possesses reasoning processes and mental structures 
that are the same as humans.20 Readers are warned that this is simply a 
figure of speech and it is not my intention to suggest that AI models are 
similar to humans.
2.1. Criminal law exam at UOW law school
The assessment task chosen for this study is the final exam for Criminal 
Law and Procedure B, a core subject in the law undergraduate programme. 
The exam is drafted based on a variety of criminal law topics covered in

this subject, including strict liability offences, public order offences, prop­
erty offences, criminal defences (e.g. self-defence) and sentencing 
offenders.
Students have three primary sources of data that they can use to prepare 
for the exam: lectures, practice seminar questions discussed in tutorials, and 
reading materials, including their textbook. The exam marking criteria 
include the students’ knowledge of relevant legal principles, ability to identify 
relevant facts, legal critical analysis, and persuasive and clear arguments.
The format and content of the exam at UOW School of Law varies each 
year. This year, the exam comprised two questions. The first question pre­
sented a problem scenario with two sub-questions (Q1-1 and Q1-2), each 
associated with different aspects of criminal law. The second question 
(Q2) consisted of a set of short answer questions and a brief essay of approxi­
mately 300 words. The exam was worth a total of 60 marks, accounting for 
60% of the course grade. Question 1 (Q1-1 and Q1-2) was allocated 50 
marks, while Question 2 was worth 10 marks.
The inclusion of both ‘problem scenarios’ and ‘open-ended questions’ in 
the assessment made it an appropriate tool for evaluating the capabilities of 
GenAI. Problem scenarios are essential in some law courses, as they demand 
extensive critical analysis and test the student’s ability to apply theoretical 
legal knowledge to hypothetical scenarios.21 While there is no prescribed 
length for problem scenarios, they typically provide the context and details 
of the offence and can range from 500 to 1000 words.
Addressing problem scenarios in law involves four key components:22
. Issue: Identify the legal question and the elements that must be proven. 
For example, determine what elements must be proven for a self- 
defence claim. For each element, students must address the following:
. Law (legal knowledge): Cite the legal rules and principles associated with 
that legal element as well as their authoritative sources, such as specific 
sections of the relevant legislation or case law.
. Application: In this step, students are required to apply the relevant legal 
rules to the case’s facts. They must identify the relevant facts and argue 
how these facts may be used to either support or undermine the legal prin­
ciples. This involves considering both sides of the argument and critically 
evaluating the strengths and weaknesses of the case. This step demands a 
high level of legal reasoning and critical analysis, which students often find 
challenging.

Unlike the problem scenario, the short answer questions in this exam did

2.2. Generating AI papers
Two important variables must be determined when generating AI responses: 
the AI engine (GenAI model) and the prompt methods (including what is 
known as “prompt engineering”). 
2.2.1. AI engines
All evaluations involving the selection of the AI engines were based on their 
availability and accessibility in October – November 2023. Since then, these 
models have been updated and are more accessible to the public. In Section 6 
Update, I will review a more recent version (May 2024) of one of the AI 
models originally selected.
The two AI companies chosen for this task were OpenAI and Google. This 
selection was based on several reasons: one factor was that this study aimed 
to evaluate the likelihood that students would use GenAI for their assign­
ments; consequently, these GenAI engines were selected based on their 
public perception, accessibility, and the fact that they were free to use.23
Additionally, the study seeks to generate the highest quality responses poss­
ible: evaluations at that time suggested that GPT-4 had the highest 
performance.24

2.2.2. Generating AI exam answers and prompt engineering
The first five papers were generated on the assumption that students were 
using GenAI to produce answers in cases where the use of AI was prohibited. 
It was also assumed that students who chose to use GenAI would put in the 
minimum effort for prompt engineering. This meant that no instructions 
were provided to the GenAI models and that only the exam question 
(excluding the cover page) was copied into the model. Nevertheless, the 
exam question itself contained specific instructions about the expected 
content of the answer. Since students are more likely to use accessible 
models, only the front-end models were used for this investigation. Further­
more, due to the variability in answer quality for the same question observed 
during test runs, two papers were generated using GPT-4 – Frontend and 
two papers were generated using GPT-3.5 – Frontend. Only one paper was 
generated by Google Bard since its outputs in the test runs were much 
poorer than those of the OpenAI models.
The second set of five papers was generated to assess the capabilities of 
different GenAI models as well as to attempt to receive the highest possible 
grade. In order to achieve the highest possible mark, each of the second set of 
five papers was provided with specific instructions on how to formulate the

in separate exams. The Multistate Performance Test (3 hours) has a weight­

candidates’ fundamental lawyering skills. Candidates are provided with the 
relevant legal sources (e.g. legislation) during the exam. The Multistate

3. Results and discussion
One of the limitations of this study is the potential impact of the tutors’ sub­
jective marking on the results. The following discussion assumes that tutors 
evaluated all papers in a consistent fashion and that if a paper had been 
marked by a different tutor, the grade would have remained the same or 
at least been relatively similar. Furthermore, as there are restrictions on 
sharing the results of the students’ exams, this study is permitted to reference 
only the average and median marks for each of the two exam questions, as 
well as their total exam marks.
3.1. Exam grades
This section provides a comparative summary of the preliminary results of 
the grades obtained by the AI-generated papers and the students’ papers; a 
more detailed analysis will be presented in Sections 3.2–3.10.
The grades for AI-generated papers varied significantly: the lowest mark 
given was 17 out of 60 (28.3% of the total mark), while the highest mark 
given was 47 (78.3% of the total mark). For Q2 (scored out of 10), grades 
ranged from 7 to 9, which represents a difference of 2 marks (20% of the 
weight of Q2) between the lowest and highest grades. The greatest impact 
on the exam outcomes was in the grades for Q1 (scored out of 50), with 
the lowest being 10 (20% of the weight of Q1) and the highest being 39 
(78% of the weight of Q1), equating to a difference of 58% of the weight 
of Q1.
The first set of five papers is composed of AI papers that were not pro­
vided with any instructions or legal content beyond what was provided in 
the exam question. GPT-4 – Frontend was used for Papers 1 and 2, while 
GPT-3.5 – Frontend was used for Papers 3 and 4. Although the prompts 
for these papers were identical, there was a seven-mark difference between

a failing grade with 26 and 21 marks out of 60, respectively, while Papers 
2 and 4 received passing grades of 33 and 30 marks, respectively. This

3.3. Structure
Students are recommended to follow the Issue, Law, Application, and Con­
clusion (ILAC) approach when answering a problem question. No instruc­
tions were provided to the models that generated the first five papers, so 
the lack of an ILAC approach was expected. However, the two papers that 
passed the exam (Papers 2 and 4) showed some attempt at ILAC. Further­
more, Paper 5’s (Google Bard) attempt at ILAC was good, and therefore 
the best among the first five papers.
Papers 6–8 included indirect guides to the ILAC approach, such as instruc­
tions on how to address each legal element individually as well as the appli­
cation of relevant legal principles. In Papers 9 and 10, however, ILAC was 
explicitly described, including an explanation of all four components and 
the issues that needed to be proved. The ILAC quality for Papers 6–8 was 
very high, whereas the ILAC quality for Papers 9 and 10 was excellent and 
exceeded expectations. This is clear evidence that providing step-by-step 
instructions for methods such as ILAC to GenAI models is effective.
3.4. Expression
In general, the expression in all AI papers was clear, with no parts that a 
reader would have found difficult to follow or confusing. Further, while 
the quality of writing varied across the papers, there were no significant 
differences between them.

interview, noting that they found ‘really inappropriate expressions and 
phrases’ and, on some occasions, could not understand the discussion. 
They specifically pointed out an example where the AI used a common 
legal phrase followed by introducing an alternative to that phrase.56

3.6. Bridging knowledge gaps: the significance of internet access for 
GenAI legal tasks
GenAI models have a cut-off date: specifically, the data used to train these 
models are only collected up to a specific point in time. Consequently, any 
knowledge or events occurring after this date would not have been included 
in their dataset. Additionally, not all publicly available data on the internet is 
used to train these models, resulting in certain knowledge gaps. Conse­
quently, internet access is crucial for some tasks as it can fill these gaps 
and provide the models with the most up-to-date information.
Given that Papers 5 (Google Bard) and 6 (GPT-4 + Bing Browser) were 
not provided with any legal content (case law) other than those in the 
exam question (legislation), and instead had access to the internet, it is 
important to examine how they attempted to fill their legal knowledge 
gaps.

3.9. Comparing Q1 and Q2
Question 2 consisted of three parts: the first two were short answer questions, 
and the third was a mini-essay. The quality of the answers to Q2 surpassed 
those of Q1 in all AI-generated papers. Furthermore, the first five papers and 
Papers 6 and 8 failed Q1, while all the papers passed Q2 with grades that 
were, on average, higher than those achieved by the average student. This 
difference is possibly due to the simpler nature of parts 1 and 2 of Q2, 
which required mainly knowledge and identification of the relevant law 
with much less critical analysis. Additionally, GenAI models were likely 
trained on more essay-like content compared to the unique structure 
required to address legal problem scenarios. Furthermore, Q2 had a 
simpler structure compared to Q1, with three short answer questions. 
Longer prompt lengths often mean that the AI pays less attention to the 
details, which can lower the quality of the output.82 The prompts for Q2 
were generally shorter than those for Q1, as Q2 did not include the 
lengthy problem scenario. Therefore, it is reasonable to expect that the AI 
models would generate higher-quality answers for Q2.
3.10. Education and future careers
One of the major concerns of many educators regarding the application of 
language models in education is academic dishonesty.83 The outcome of 
AI-generated papers suggests that students might conceivably use Gen-AI 
for their assignments to achieve passing grades. Two of the first five AI-gen­
erated papers, which were merely copy-pasted exam papers, passed with 
marks of 30 (GPT-3.5) and 33 (GPT-4) out of 60, which equates to a 40% 
chance of passing an exam without any effort. This likelihood increases 
when students are made aware of the better-performing AI models. For 
instance, if a student knew that Google Bard’s performance was not as 
good compared to OpenAI’s models, their chance of passing increases to 
50%. With newer models presumably becoming capable of improved per­
formances, passing assessments withtout effort could be even more likely. 
However, it is important to note that the data in this study is very limited, 
and the five papers generated in this study cannot and do not represent 
the overall performance of these models.

4. Limitations
One of the main limitations of this study is that only 10 AI papers were gen­
erated, which does not provide a comprehensive picture of the limitations 
and capabilities of GenAI. Additionally, only five tutors participated in the 
study, limiting the scope of the interview results to the perspective of these 
participants. Furthermore, although all tutors reviewed the AI-generated 
papers again during the interviews, it is possible they did not recall some 
details of the papers given that the interviews were conducted two to three 
weeks after the marking period.
This study also lacks inter-rater reliability since each tutor marked a 
different AI paper; this introduces potential subjective components and 
unconscious biases, such as the influence of handwriting on the perceived 
quality of the responses.86 A key reason for not having multiple tutors mark 
the same AI paper was the fact that tutors had not been initially asked for 
consent to participate in the study; consequently, there were some ethical con­
cerns associated with the consistency of marking across tutors if their marking 
outcomes were different. Despite this, the comprehensive marking guide and

5. Implications and recommendations
There are significant implications for the use of AI in legal education and the 
field in general. Although most GenAI papers did not receive higher grades 
than students’ average, with thoughtful prompt engineering and structured 
content, some GenAI papers received higher marks, including a ‘Credit’ 
(grades between 65 and 74) and a ‘Distinction’ (grades between 75 and 
84). These results suggest that humans are not at risk of suddenly being 
replaced with AIs in the legal sector, but those who know how to utilise 
and work with AI will have an advantage.90 This study suggests that more 
significant improvements are necessary for AI to surpass many human qual­
ities. In the near future, it is unlikely that graduates will be compared against 
AI models during the employment process for jobs requiring high cognitive 
skills. Instead, they will be assessed on their ability to work with AI to com­
plete tasks more efficiently and with higher quality. Consequently, it is 
necessary to invest in Legal AI literacy to prepare students for the emerging 
job market.91
Universities need to shift the focus from building from scratch to generating 
the best first draft with AI. This study revealed significant differences in the 
performance of different AI models (GPT-4 vs. Google Bard) as well as the 
impact of prompt formulation. The study also demonstrated that if prompts 
are properly structured, AI can identify legal components that need to be 
proved as well as their relevant legal authorities (e.g. Paper 10). As such, stu­
dents must be equipped with the knowledge of how to identify the GenAI 
models that are appropriate for specific tasks and learn new skills such as 
how to organise data in their prompts.
None of the GenAI Papers demonstrated a satisfactory level of critical 
analysis, especially when asked to apply the law to facts presented in the 
problem scenario. Students also often struggle with these skills. Conse­
quently, relying on AI to generate a first draft can be risky, as students 
may lack the necessary critical analysis abilities to identify potential errors 
and areas for improvement. This suggests law schools must emphasise the 
development of critical legal analytical skills to enhance students’ overall 
legal reasoning and achieve better outcomes when collaborating with AI.
However, this shift in focus should not come at the cost of neglecting the 
teaching of relevant content and foundational knowledge in the field. A solid 
grasp of the subject matter as well as familiarity with the best practices is 
essential for students to identify errors in AI-generated content92 and

edge in the legal field.
Universities also need a comprehensive program to address the significant 
potential impact of over-reliance on AI. While the increasing performance 
of AI may result in fewer errors and higher quality work, it also poses the 
risk of fostering a dangerous level of trust, potentially diminishing the 
emphasis on human oversight.94 For example, GPT-4 tends to provide 
false information or perform tasks incorrectly ‘in ways that are more convin­
cing and believable than earlier GPT models’.95 This issue is partly due to 
GPT-4s ‘authoritative tone’ and the presentation of incorrect statements 
within the context of highly detailed and accurate information.96 Further­
more, GPT-4 exhibits ‘a tendency to hedge in its responses,’ which users 
may interpret as ‘epistemic humility’, consequently fostering trust and 
reliance on the AI model.97 This phenomenon could explain findings from 
a study that show that the use of AI assistance had a diverse impact on 
student performance.98 In this study, the worst-performing students saw sig­
nificant improvements, while the best-performing students exhibited a 
decline of approximately 20 percentile points.99 The authors of the study 
suggested that ‘access to AI might discourage effort when used as a 
crutch’;100 this phenomenon occurred even when the students were 
trained and familiar with the limitations of GenAI.101 Furthermore, a 
study from Harvard Business School also found that while GenAI can 
boost the efficiency of certain tasks, it can also cause disruptions: for

the performance of this model.

GPT-4o was evaluated using the backend model GPT-4o-2024-05-13 with 
the same prompts as Paper 10. The results showed that there was no signifi­
cant improvement in performance compared to Paper 10. Interestingly, the 
backend model used to generate Paper 10, GPT-4-1106-preview, occasion­
ally cited legal authorities more frequently in Q1-1. Throughout the 
answers, there were instances where one model exhibited better reasoning 
than the other; however, their overall critical analysis remained comparable.
6.2. Paper 12
Paper 12 was generated by using the GPT-4o–Frontend model which was 
used without any prompts, similar to how Papers 1–5 were generated. 
Paper 12 was compared directly with Papers 1 and 2, as those papers were 
generated using GPT-4, an earlier version of GPT-4o.
Paper 12 demonstrated a more organised approach compared to Papers 1 
and 2 in Q1-1. Paper 12 addressed each legal element in separate paragraphs 
rather than combining all elements of the legal matter into a single para­
graph. In terms of its other aspects, such as its capability for critical analysis 
and its citation of legal authorities, the answers were generally of the same 
quality across all papers.
Like Q1-1, Paper 12 provided a more organised response by individually 
addressing each legal element in Q1-2. Furthermore, each legal element was 
described with slightly more accuracy and detail. Paper 12 also included a

7. Conclusion
This study examines the capabilities and limitations of current GenAI 
models in the field of legal education, focusing specifically on their perform­
ance in the Criminal Law exam at the University of Wollongong. Unlike 
OpenAI’s benchmarks, which highlighted GPT-4’s impressive performance 
in the US Uniform Bar Examination, the findings in this study suggest 
that the current GenAI models are comparatively less capable when it 
comes to university-level Criminal Law exams. Thus, educators should 
avoid generalising the model benchmarks set by companies. This caution 
is not necessarily due to dishonesty on the part of the companies; rather, 
the context and methods used in testing these models may differ significantly 
from what educators find useful and relevant.
A considerable weakness of AI-generated answers was the lack of sophis­
ticated critical legal analysis. While GenAI models demonstrated a stronger 
capacity for critical analysis in short-answer and short essay questions, their 
performance declined drastically when faced with the complexity of problem 
scenarios. This level of complexity is expected in real-world legal scenarios 
and, therefore, more accurately represents the capabilities of the current 
AI models in a legal context.
These findings have significant implications for policies regarding the 
use of GenAI in universities. In law schools that ban the use of AI in assess­
ments, students may use GenAI (i.e. cheating) and would still be able to 
pass non-invigilated assessments without any genuine effort and a true 
understanding of the subject matter. As a result, these students would 
not only miss the opportunity to gain an understanding of the foundational 
concepts of their field of study but they also fail to learn how to collaborate

effectively with AI. This collaboration is likely to be an essential skill in 
their future careers, where expectations extend far beyond merely achiev­
ing a passing grade.
Conversely, in law schools where students are allowed to use GenAI in 
their assessments, the weakness of GenAI in critical analysis tasks means 
that educators should place a greater emphasis on developing critical analysis 
skills. This approach will prepare graduates to collaborate with AI tools and 
leverage AI-driven outputs in their professional careers. However, this shift 
will be challenging, especially in the context of traditional academic concepts 
such as originality. Specifically, many assessment policies require students to 
complete their assignments independently as part of their original work. 
However, AI-generated answers allow students to potentially redirect their 
efforts: instead of drafting an essay from scratch, students can now focus 
on critically analysing and refining the AI-generated outputs. In light of 
recent advancements in GenAI, it may be time to reconsider the definition 
of originality to include the evaluation and enhancement of AI-generated 
content.
As the landscape of legal research and education continuously evolves, we 
must prepare to engage with and critically assess emerging concepts that may 
be useful in education. We must engage in thoughtful discourse about these 
complex issues before deciding on a course of action. For example, the 
definition of concepts such as academic originality may have far-reaching 
implications for students’ future career opportunities.
The results and implications of this study must be interpreted cautiously 
due to several inherent limitations. These include the limited sample size of 
AI-generated papers, the potential bias from the tutors’ subjective marking, 
and the influence of the specific Criminal Law exam format at UOW on the 
outputs of the GenAI models. Given these limitations, and in light of the 
results of this paper, this study encourages further research across different 
jurisdictions and prompts an exploration of the implications of the use of 
GenAI in legal contexts. Such research and discussion are crucial to ensuring 
that students are equipped to thrive in an era where they risk becoming obso­
lete without proper preparation.
